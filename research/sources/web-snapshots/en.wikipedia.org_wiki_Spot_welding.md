Type of electric resistance welding

</div>

**Spot welding** (or **resistance spot
welding**<sup><a href="#cite_note-1" id="mwCg"><span id="mwCw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwDA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>1<span id="mwDQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>) is a type of
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_resistance_welding"
id="mwDg" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Electric resistance welding">electric
resistance welding</a> used to weld various sheet metal products,
through a process in which contacting metal surface points are joined by
the heat obtained from resistance to
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current" id="mwDw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Electric current">electric current</a>.

The process uses two shaped
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_copper_alloys" id="mwEQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="List of copper alloys">copper alloy</a>
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrode" id="mwEg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Electrode">electrodes</a> to concentrate
welding current into a small "spot" and to simultaneously clamp the
sheets together. Work-pieces are held together under pressure exerted by
electrodes. Typically the sheets are in the <span id="mwEw"
about="#mwt5" typeof="mw:Transclusion"
mw="{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;convert&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Convert&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;0.5&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;to&quot;},&quot;3&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;3&quot;},&quot;4&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;mm&quot;},&quot;abbr&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;on&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}">0.5
to 3</span><span typeof="mw:Entity"
about="#mwt5"> </span><span about="#mwt5">mm (0.020 to
0.118</span><span typeof="mw:Entity"
about="#mwt5"> </span><span about="#mwt5">in)</span> thickness range.
Forcing a large current through the spot will melt the metal and form
the weld. The attractive feature of spot welding is that a large amount
of energy can be delivered to the spot in a very short time
(approximately 10–100
milliseconds).<sup><a href="#cite_note-2" id="mwFA"><span id="mwFQ"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwFg"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>2<span id="mwFw"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> This permits the welding
to occur without excessive heating of the remainder of the sheet.

The amount of heat (energy) delivered to the spot is determined by the
resistance between the electrodes and the magnitude and duration of the
current.<sup><a href="#cite_note-3" id="mwGQ"><span id="mwGg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwGw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>3<span id="mwHA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The amount of energy is
chosen to match the sheet's material properties, its thickness, and type
of electrodes. Applying too little energy will not melt the metal or
will make a poor weld. Applying too much energy will melt too much
metal, eject molten material, and make a hole rather than a
weld.<sup><a href="#cite_note-4" id="mwHQ"><span id="mwHg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwHw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>4<span id="mwIA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> Another feature of spot
welding is that the energy delivered to the spot can be controlled to
produce reliable welds.

Spot welding involves three stages; the first of which involves the
electrodes being brought to the surface of the metal and applying a
slight amount of pressure. The current from the electrodes is then
applied briefly after which the current is removed but the electrodes
remain in place for the material to cool. Weld times range from
0.01<span id="mwKA" typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>sec to
0.63<span id="mwKQ" typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>sec depending on the
thickness of the metal, the electrode force and the diameter of the
electrodes
themselves.<sup><span typeof="mw:Entity">\[</span>*<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span
title="Specific figures not backed up (May 2017)">citation
needed</span></a>*<span typeof="mw:Entity">\]</span></sup>

The equipment used in the spot welding process consists of tool holders
and electrodes. The tool holders function as a mechanism to hold the
electrodes firmly in place and also support optional water hoses that
cool the electrodes during welding. Tool holding methods include a
paddle-type, light duty, universal, and regular offset. The electrodes
generally are made of a low resistance alloy, usually copper, and are
designed in many different shapes and sizes depending on the application
needed.

The two materials being welded together are known as the workpieces and
must conduct electricity. The width of the workpieces is limited by the
throat length of the welding apparatus and ranges typically from
<span id="mwLg" about="#mwt11" typeof="mw:Transclusion"
mw="{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;convert&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Convert&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;5&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;to&quot;},&quot;3&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;50&quot;},&quot;4&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;in&quot;},&quot;5&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;cm&quot;},&quot;sigfig&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;2&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}">5
to 50 inches (13 to 130</span><span typeof="mw:Entity"
about="#mwt11"> </span><span about="#mwt11">cm)</span>. Workpiece
thickness can range from <span id="mwLw" about="#mwt12"
typeof="mw:Transclusion"
mw="{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;convert&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Convert&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;0.008&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;to&quot;},&quot;3&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;1.25&quot;},&quot;4&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;in&quot;},&quot;5&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;mm&quot;},&quot;sigfig&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;2&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}">0.008
to 1.25 inches (0.20 to 32</span><span typeof="mw:Entity"
about="#mwt12"> </span><span about="#mwt12">mm)</span>.<sup><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-5" id="mwMA"><span id="mwMQ"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwMg"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>5<span id="mwMw"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

After the current is removed from the workpiece, it is cooled via the
coolant holes in the center of the electrodes. Both water and a brine
solution may be used as coolants in spot welding mechanisms.

In the case of resistance spot welding, there are two main parts of the
tooling system, the features of which fundamentally influence the whole
process: the gun and its type, and the size and shape of the electrode.
In such application, where the gun layout should be as rigid as possible
due to the high applying forces (e.g. welding of thick materials), the
C-type gun is widely used. As well as the high resulting rigidity, this
arrangement leads to a high tooling flexibility, as the motion of the
electrodes is collinear. Unlike the C-type, the so-called X-type
arrangement provides less rigidity, although the reachable workspace is
far larger than with the C-type, thus this layout is very common, where
thin and flat objects are being processed (e.g. manufacturing of floor
pan or roof panel). However, it offers less flexibility in terms of
tooling, because the paths of the moving electrodes are not collinear
(like the tips of a scissor), so a dome-shaped electrode tip should be
used.

Electrodes used in spot welding can vary greatly with different
applications. Each tool style has a different purpose. Radius style
electrodes are used for high heat applications, electrodes with a
truncated tip for high pressure, eccentric electrodes for welding
corners, offset eccentric tips for reaching into corners and small
spaces, and finally offset truncated for reaching into the workpiece
itself.

The spot welding process tends to harden the material, causing it to
warp. This reduces the material's fatigue strength, and may stretch the
material as well as
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annealing_(metallurgy)" id="mwOg"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Annealing (metallurgy)">anneal</a> it. The physical effects of
spot welding include internal cracking, surface cracks and a bad
appearance. The crack around the weld nugget will be extended under an
external load or fatigue to produce a different type of
failure.<sup><a href="#cite_note-6" id="mwOw"><span id="mwPA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwPQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>6<span id="mwPg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-7" id="mwPw"><span id="mwQA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwQQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>7<span id="mwQg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The chemical properties
affected include the metal's internal resistance and its corrosive
properties.

Welding times are often very short, which can cause problems with the
electrodes—they cannot move fast enough to keep the material clamped.
Welding controllers will use a double pulse to get around this problem.
During the first pulse, the electrode contact may not be able to make a
good weld. The first pulse will soften the metal. During the pause
between the two pulses, the electrodes will come closer and make better
contact.

During spot welding, the large electric current induces a large magnetic
field, and the electric current and magnetic field interact with each
other to produce a large magnetic force field too, which drives the
melted metal to move very fast at a velocity up to 0.5<span id="mwRQ"
typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>m/s. As such, the heat energy distribution in
spot welding could be dramatically changed by the fast motion of the
melted metal.<sup><a href="#cite_note-8" id="mwRg"><span id="mwRw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwSA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>8<span id="mwSQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-9" id="mwSg"><span id="mwSw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwTA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>9<span id="mwTQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-10" id="mwTg"><span id="mwTw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwUA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>10<span id="mwUQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The fast motion in spot
welding can be observed with high-speed
photography.<sup><a href="#cite_note-11" id="mwUg"><span id="mwUw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwVA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>11<span id="mwVQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

The basic spot welder consists of a power supply, an energy storage unit
(e.g., a capacitor bank), a switch, a welding transformer, and the
welding electrodes. The energy storage element allows the welder to
deliver high instantaneous power levels. If the power demands are not
high, then the energy storage element isn't needed. The switch causes
the stored energy to be dumped into the welding transformer. The welding
transformer steps down the voltage and steps up the current. An
important feature of the transformer is it reduces the current level
that the switch must handle. The welding electrodes are part of the
transformer's secondary circuit. There is also a control box that
manages the switch and may monitor the welding electrode voltage or
current.

The resistance presented to the welder is
complicated.<sup><a href="#cite_note-12" id="mwWA"><span id="mwWQ"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwWg"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwWw"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> There is the resistance
of secondary winding, the cables, and the welding electrodes. There is
also the contact resistance between the welding electrodes and the
workpiece. There is the resistance of the workpieces, and the contact
resistance between the workpieces.

At the beginning of the weld, the contact resistances are usually high,
so most of the initial energy will be dissipated there. That heat and
the clamping force will soften and smooth out the material at the
electrode-material interface and make better contact (that is, lower the
contact resistance). Consequently, more electrical energy will go into
the workpiece and the junction resistance of the two workpieces. As
electrical energy is delivered to the weld and causes the temperature to
rise, the electrodes and the workpiece are conducting that heat away.
The goal is to apply enough energy so that a portion of material within
the spot melts without having the entire spot melt. The perimeter of the
spot will conduct away much heat and keep the perimeter at a lower
temperature. The interior of the spot has less heat conducted away, so
it melts first. If the welding current is applied too long, the entire
spot melts, the material runs out or otherwise fails, and the "weld"
becomes a hole.

The voltage needed for welding depends on the resistance of the material
to be welded, the sheet thickness and desired size of the nugget. When
welding a common combination like 1.0 + 1.0<span id="mwXg"
typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>mm sheet steel, the voltage between the
electrodes is only about 1.5<span id="mwXw" typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>V
at the start of the weld but can fall as low as 1<span id="mwYA"
typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>V at the end of the weld. This decrease in
voltage results from the reduction in resistance caused by the workpiece
melting. The open circuit voltage from the transformer is higher than
this, typically in the 5 to 22 volt
range.<sup><a href="#cite_note-13" id="mwYQ"><span id="mwYg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwYw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>13<span id="mwZA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

The
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance" id="mwZg"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Electrical resistance">resistance</a> of the weld spot changes as
it flows and liquefies. Modern welding equipment can monitor and adjust
the weld in real time to ensure a consistent weld. The equipment may
seek to control different variables during the weld, such as current,
voltage, power, or energy.

Welder sizes range from 5 to 500<span id="mwaA"
typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>kVA.<sup><a href="#cite_note-14" id="mwaQ"><span id="mwag"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwaw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>14<span id="mwbA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> Micro spot welders, used
in a variety of industries, can go down to 1.5<span id="mwbQ"
typeof="mw:Entity"> </span>kVA or less for precision welding needs.

It is common for a spray of molten metal droplets (sparks) to be ejected
from the area of the weld during the process.

Resistance spot welding generates no bright arc, so UV protection is not
required. <a
href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration"
id="mwcA" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Occupational Safety and Health Administration">OSHA</a> requires
transparent face shields or goggles for splatter protection, but does
not require any filter
lens.<sup><a href="#cite_note-15" id="mwcQ"><span id="mwcg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwcw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>15<span id="mwdA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

Spot welding is typically used when welding particular types of
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_metal" id="mwfQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Sheet metal">sheet metal</a>,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welded_wire_mesh" id="mwfg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Welded wire mesh">welded wire mesh</a> or
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weldmesh" id="mwfw"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Weldmesh">wire mesh</a>.
Thicker <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_(metal)" id="mwgA"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Plate (metal)">stock</a> is
more difficult to spot weld because the heat flows into the surrounding
metal more easily. Spot welding can be easily identified on many sheet
metal goods, such as metal buckets.
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_alloy" id="mwgQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Aluminium alloy">Aluminium alloys</a> can be
spot welded, but their much higher
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity" id="mwgg"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Thermal conductivity">thermal conductivity</a> and
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity"
id="mwgw" class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Electrical conductivity">electrical conductivity</a> requires
higher welding currents. This requires larger, more powerful, and more
expensive welding
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer" id="mwhA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Transformer">transformers</a>.

Perhaps the most common application of spot welding is in the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_industry" id="mwhg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Automotive industry">automobile manufacturing
industry</a>, where it is used almost universally to weld the sheet
metal to form a car. Spot welders can also be
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_welding" id="mwhw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Robot welding">completely automated</a>, and
many of the industrial
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot" id="mwiA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Robot">robots</a> found on
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line" id="mwiQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Assembly line">assembly lines</a> are spot
welders (the other major use for robots being painting).

Spot welding is also used in the orthodontist's clinic, where
small-scale spot welding equipment is used when resizing metal "molar
bands" used in
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodontics" id="mwiw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Orthodontics">orthodontics</a>.

Another application is spot welding straps to
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel–cadmium_battery" id="mwjQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Nickel–cadmium battery">nickel–cadmium</a>,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel–metal_hydride_battery"
id="mwjg" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Nickel–metal hydride battery">nickel–metal hydride</a> or
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion" id="mwjw"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Lithium-ion">Lithium-ion
battery</a> cells to make batteries. The cells are joined by spot
welding thin nickel straps to the battery terminals. Spot welding can
keep the battery from getting too hot, as might happen if conventional
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldering" id="mwkA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Soldering">soldering</a> were done.

Good design practice must always allow for adequate accessibility.
Connecting surfaces should be free of contaminants such as scale, oil,
and dirt, to ensure quality welds. Metal thickness is generally not a
factor in determining good welds.

## Modifications

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spot_welding&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Modifications")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

*Projection welding* is a modification of spot welding in which the weld
is localized by means of raised sections, or projections, on one or both
of the workpieces to be joined. Heat is concentrated at the projections,
which permits the welding of heavier sections or the closer spacing of
welds. The projections can also serve as a means of positioning the
workpieces. Projection welding is often used to
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stud_welding" id="mwlQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Stud welding">weld studs</a>, nuts, and other
threaded machine parts to metal plate. It is also frequently used to
join crossed wires and bars. This is another high-production process,
and multiple projection welds can be arranged by suitable designing and
jigging.<sup><a href="#cite_note-16" id="mwlg"><span id="mwlw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwmA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>16<span id="mwmQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

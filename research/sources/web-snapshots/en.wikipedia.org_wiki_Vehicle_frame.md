A **vehicle frame**, also historically known as its
*<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chassis" id="mwFA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chassis">chassis</a>*, is the main supporting
structure of a
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_vehicle" id="mwFQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Motor vehicle">motor vehicle</a> to which all
other components are attached, comparable to the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton" id="mwFg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Skeleton">skeleton</a> of an organism.

Until the 1930s, virtually every car had a structural frame separate
from its body, known as
*<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-on-frame" id="mwGQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Body-on-frame">body-on-frame</a>* construction.
Both mass production of completed vehicles by a manufacturer using this
method, epitomized by the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_T" id="mwGg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Ford Model T">Ford Model T</a>, and supply of
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_chassis" id="mwGw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Rolling chassis">rolling chassis</a> to
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coachbuilder" id="mwHA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Coachbuilder">coachbuilders</a> for both mass
production (as by
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_Body" id="mwHQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Fisher Body">Fisher Body</a> in the United
States) and to smaller firms (such as
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooper_(coachbuilder)" id="mwHg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Hooper (coachbuilder)">Hooper</a>) for bespoke
bodies and interiors was practiced.

By the 1960s, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unibody" id="mwIA"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Unibody">unibody</a>
construction in passenger cars had become common, and the trend towards
building unibody passenger cars continued over the ensuing
decades.<sup><a href="#cite_note-pros-and-cons-1" id="mwIQ"><span id="mwIg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwIw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>1<span id="mwJA"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

Nearly all <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truck" id="mwJg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Truck">trucks</a> and buses, and most
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickup_truck" id="mwJw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Pickup truck">pickups</a> continue to use a
separate frame as their chassis.

</div>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

## Functions

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Functions")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

The main functions of a frame in a motor vehicle
are:<sup><a href="#cite_note-2" id="mwKw"><span id="mwLA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwLQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>2<span id="mwLg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

1.  <span id="mwMA">To support the vehicle's mechanical components and
    body</span>
2.  <span id="mwMQ">To deal with static and dynamic loads without undue
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_(engineering)"
    id="mwMg" rel="mw:WikiLink"
    title="Deflection (engineering)">deflection</a> or distortion</span>

These include:

- <span id="mwOA">Weight of the body, passengers, and cargo
  loads.</span>
- <span id="mwOQ">Vertical and
  <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsional" id="mwOg"
  class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Torsional">torsional</a>
  twisting transmitted by going over uneven surfaces</span>
- <span id="mwOw">Transverse lateral forces caused by road conditions,
  side wind, and steering of the vehicle</span>
- <span id="mwPA">Torque from the engine and transmission</span>
- <span id="mwPQ">Longitudinal
  <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensile_force" id="mwPg"
  class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Tensile force">tensile
  forces</a> from starting and acceleration, as well as compression from
  braking</span>
- <span id="mwPw">Sudden impacts from collisions</span>

</div>

Typically, the material used to construct vehicle chassis and frames
include <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel#Carbon" id="mwSw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Steel">carbon steel</a> for strength or
aluminum alloys to achieve a more lightweight construction. In the case
of a separate chassis, the frame is made up of
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_element" id="mwTA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Structural element">structural elements</a>
called the *rails* or
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_(structure)" id="mwTg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Beam (structure)"><em>beams</em></a>. These are
ordinarily made of steel *channel* sections by folding, rolling, or
pressing steel plate.

There are three main designs for these. If the material is folded twice,
an open-ended cross-section, either C-shaped or hat-shaped (U-shaped),
results. "Boxed" frames contain closed chassis rails, either by welding
them up or by using premanufactured
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollow_structural_section"
id="mwUg" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Hollow structural section">metal
tubing</a>.

<div id="mwUw" class="section" mw-section-id="3"
aria-labelledby="C-Shaped">

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

### C-Shaped

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: C-Shaped")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

By far the most common, the C-channel rail has been used on nearly every
type of vehicle at one time or
another.<sup><span typeof="mw:Entity">\[</span>*<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span
title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2020)">citation
needed</span></a>*<span typeof="mw:Entity">\]</span></sup> It is made by
taking a flat piece of steel (usually ranging in thickness from 1/8" to
3/16", but up to 1/2" or more in some heavy-duty
trucks<sup><a href="#cite_note-3" id="mwVw"><span id="mwWA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwWQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>3<span id="mwWg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-4" id="mwWw"><span id="mwXA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwXQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>4<span id="mwXg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>) and rolling both sides
over to form a C-shaped beam running the length of the vehicle.
C-channel is typically more flexible than (fully) boxed of the same
gauge.

</div>

<div id="mwXw" class="section" mw-section-id="4" aria-labelledby="Hat">

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

### Hat

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Hat")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

Hat frames resemble a "U" and may be either right-side-up or inverted,
with the open area facing down. They are not commonly used due to
weakness and a propensity to rust. However, they can be found on
1936–1954 <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet" id="mwYQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chevrolet">Chevrolet</a> cars and some
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studebaker" id="mwYg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Studebaker">Studebakers</a>.

Abandoned for a while, the hat frame regained popularity when companies
started welding it to the bottom of unibody cars, effectively creating a
boxed frame.

</div>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

### Boxed

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Boxed")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

Originally, boxed frames were made by welding two matching C-rails
together to form a rectangular tube. Modern techniques, however, use a
process similar to making C-rails in that a piece of steel is bent into
four sides and then welded where both ends meet.

In the 1960s, the boxed frames of conventional American cars were
spot-welded in multiple places down the seam; when turned into NASCAR
"stock car" racers, the box was continuously welded from end to end for
extra strength.

</div>

<div id="mwaw" class="section" mw-section-id="6"
aria-labelledby="Design_features">

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

### Design features

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Design features")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

While appearing at first glance as a simple form made of metal, frames
encounter significant stress and are built accordingly. The first issue
addressed is "beam height", or the height of the vertical side of a
frame. The taller the frame, the better it can resist vertical flex when
force is applied to the top of the frame. This is the reason
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truck" id="mwbQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Truck">semi-trucks</a> have taller frame rails
than other vehicles instead of just being thicker.

As looks, ride quality, and handling became more important to consumers,
new shapes were incorporated into frames. The most visible of these are
arches and kick-ups. Instead of running straight over both
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axle" id="mwbw" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Axle">axles</a>, arched frames sit lower—roughly level with their
axles—and curve up over the axles and then back down on the other side
for bumper placement. Kick-ups do the same thing without curving down on
the other side and are more common on the front ends.

Another feature are the tapered rails that narrow vertically or
horizontally in front of a vehicle's cabin. This is done mainly on
trucks to save weight and slightly increase room for the engine since
the front of the vehicle does not bear as much load as the back. Design
developments include frames that use multiple shapes in the same frame
rail. For example, some pickup trucks have a boxed frame in front of the
cab, shorter, narrower rails underneath the cab, and regular C-rails
under the bed.

On perimeter frames, the areas where the rails connect from front to
center and center to rear are weak compared to regular frames, so that
section is boxed in, creating what are called "torque boxes".

</div>

#### Ladder frame

Named for its resemblance to a ladder, the ladder frame is one of the
oldest, simplest, and most frequently used under-body, separate
chassis/frame designs. It consists of two symmetrical beams, rails, or
channels, running the length of the vehicle, connected by several
transverse cross-members. Initially seen on almost all vehicles, the
ladder frame was gradually phased out on cars in favor of perimeter
frames and unitized body construction. It is now seen mainly on large
trucks. This design offers good side impact resistance because of its
continuous rails from front to rear, but poor resistance to torsion or
warping if simple, perpendicular cross-members are used. The vehicle's
overall height will be greater due to the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_pan" id="mwfQ"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Floor pan">floor pan</a>
sitting above the frame instead of inside it.

#### Backbone tube

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Backbone tube")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

A <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backbone_chassis" id="mwhw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Backbone chassis">backbone chassis</a> is a
type of automotive construction with chassis that is similar to the
body-on-frame design. Instead of a relatively flat, ladder-like
structure with two longitudinal, parallel frame rails, it consists of a
central, strong tubular backbone (usually rectangular in cross-section)
that carries the power-train and connects the front and rear suspension
attachment structures. Although the backbone is frequently drawn upward
into, and mostly *above* the floor of the vehicle, the body is still
placed on or over (sometimes straddling) this structure from above.

#### X-frame

An X-frame is built generally in the shape of the letter X, beginning in
its simplest form with two frame rails parallel to one another in the
engine compartment, crossing (or joining) in the middle, then returning
to parallel at or after the rear axle. The purpose of the design was to
allow the floor pan to be placed lower than had been possible sitting
atop a full ladder frame. Centerline humps, however, provided for the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_train" id="mwkA"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Power train">power
train</a> and central crossmember, intruded into the cabin space.

The X-frame varied in stiffness depending on the gauge and proportion of
its cross-section, but could be rigid when heavy enough. It was widely
used, as in the exclusive
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_W189" id="mwkg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Mercedes-Benz W189">Mercedes-Benz 300</a>
"Adenaeur" limousines, and for some full-sized GM cars of the late 1950s
and early 1960s.

A shortcoming was weakness to side-impact, resulting in the addition of
side rails (that still allowed a recessed cabin), spurring development
of the perimeter
frame.<sup><a href="#cite_note-5" id="mwlA"><span id="mwlQ"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwlg"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>5<span id="mwlw"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

#### Perimeter frame

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Perimeter frame")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

<figure id="mwmQ" class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb">
<a

data-file-type="bitmap"
srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Anadol_FW11_prototype_chassis.jpg/500px-Anadol_FW11_prototype_chassis.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x"

Similar to a ladder frame, but the middle sections of the frame rails
sit outboard of the front and rear rails, routed around the passenger
footwells, inside the rocker and sill panels. This allowed the floor pan
to be lowered, especially the passenger footwells, lowering the
passengers' seating height and thereby reducing both the roof-line and
overall vehicle height, as well as the center of gravity, thus improving
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_handling" id="mwnw"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Vehicle handling">handling</a> and road-holding in passenger
cars.

This became the prevalent design for
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-on-frame" id="mwoQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Body-on-frame">body-on-frame</a> cars in the
United States, but not in the rest of the world, until the unibody
gained popularity. For example,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Motor_Car_Company"
id="mwog" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Hudson Motor Car Company">Hudson</a>
introduced this construction on their third generation <a
href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Commodore#Third_generation"
id="mwow" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Hudson Commodore">Commodore
models</a> in 1948. This frame type allowed for
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_year" id="mwpA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Model year">annual model changes</a>, and lower
cars, introduced in the 1950s to increase sales – without costly
structural changes.

The
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Panther_platform" id="mwpg"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Ford Panther platform">Ford Panther
platform</a>, discontinued in 2011, was one of the last perimeter frame
passenger car platforms in the United
States.<sup><a href="#cite_note-pros-and-cons-1" id="mwpw"><span id="mwqA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwqQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>1<span id="mwqg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The fourth to seventh
generation
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Corvette" id="mwqw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chevrolet Corvette">Chevrolet Corvette</a> used
a perimeter frame integrated with an internal skeleton that serves as a
clamshell.

In addition to a lowered roof, the perimeter frame allows lower seating
positions when that is desirable, and offers better safety in the event
of a side impact. However, the design lacks stiffness because the
transition areas from front to center and center to rear reduce beam and
torsional resistance and is used in combination with torque boxes and
soft suspension settings.

#### Platform frame

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Platform frame")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

</div>

<span id="mwrg" class="mw-empty-elt" about="#mwt26"
typeof="mw:Transclusion"
mw="{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;main&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Main&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Platform chassis&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}"></span>

<div id="mwrw" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable" role="note"
about="#mwt26">

Main article: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_chassis"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Platform chassis">Platform chassis</a>

</div>

This is a modification of the perimeter frame, or of the backbone frame,
in which the passenger compartment floor, and sometimes the luggage
compartment floor, have been integrated into the frame as loadbearing
parts for strength and rigidity. The
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_metal" id="mwsQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Sheet metal">sheet metal</a> used to assemble
the components needs to be
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamping_(metalworking)"
id="mwsg" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Stamping (metalworking)">stamped</a>
with ridges and hollows to give it strength.

Platform chassis were used on several successful European cars, most
notably the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkswagen_Beetle" id="mwtA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Volkswagen Beetle">Volkswagen Beetle</a>, where
it was called "body-on-pan" construction. Another German example are the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_Ponton" id="mwtQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Mercedes-Benz Ponton">Mercedes-Benz
"Ponton"</a> cars of the 1950s and
1960s,<sup><a href="#cite_note-6" id="mwtg"><span id="mwtw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwuA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>6<span id="mwuQ"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> where it was called a
"frame floor" in English-language advertisements.

The French <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renault_4" id="mwuw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Renault 4">Renault 4</a>, of which over eight
million were made, also used a platform frame. The frame of the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citroën_2CV" id="mwvA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Citroën 2CV">Citroën 2CV</a> used a minimal
interpretation of a platform chassis under its body.

Originally known as a "tubular frame", the space frame (also
"spaceframe") utilizes tubular steel, alloy, or carbon fibre to create a
load-bearing three-dimensional skeleton, to which the suspension,
engine, and body panels are attached. As the body panels have limited or
no structural function, geometry is used to maximize rigidity and
minimize weight, frequently employing triangles where all the forces in
each strut are either tensile or compressive. The lack of bending forces
allows members to be kept to a minimum weight and cross-section.

The first true spaceframe chassis were designed and produced in the
1930s by
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller" id="mwzw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Buckminster Fuller">Buckminster Fuller</a> and
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bushnell_Stout" id="mw0A"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="William Bushnell Stout">William Bushnell
Stout</a>, who understood the theory supporting them from either
architecture or aircraft design, resulting in the bus-like
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dymaxion_car" id="mw0Q"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Dymaxion car">Dymaxion</a> and
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stout_Scarab" id="mw0g"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Stout Scarab">Stout Scarab</a>.<sup><a href="#cite_note-7" id="mw0w"><span id="mw1A"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mw1Q"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>7<span id="mw1g"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> Maximizing space while
minimizing weight were the goals.

With its high strength-to-weight ratio, the space frame was adapted to
automobile racing following World War II. The 1951
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaguar_C-Type" id="mw2A"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Jaguar C-Type">Jaguar C-Type</a>
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_car_racing" id="mw2Q"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Sports car racing">racing sports car</a>
utilized a lightweight, multi-tubular, triangulated frame over which an
aerodynamic aluminum body was crafted. The form saw mass production with
the 1954 introduction of the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_W198" id="mw2g"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Mercedes-Benz W198">Mercedes-Benz 300 SL</a> "Gullwing" sports
car, the fastest road-going automobile of its day. The car's
exceptionally high <a
href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_automotive_design#sill"
id="mw2w" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Glossary of automotive design">sills</a> made conventional doors
impractical, spawning the model's iconic
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gullwing_doors" id="mw3A"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Gullwing doors">gullwing
doors</a>.

In 1994, the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audi_A8" id="mw3g"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Audi A8">Audi A8</a> was the first mass-market
car with an aluminium chassis, made feasible by integrating an aluminium
space-frame into the bodywork. Audi A8 models have since used this
construction method co-developed with
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoa" id="mw3w"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Alcoa">Alcoa</a>, and marketed as the *Audi
Space
Frame*.<sup><a href="#cite_note-nytalcoa-8" id="mw4Q"><span id="mw4g"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mw4w"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>8<span id="mw5A"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

A tubular frame that is not load-bearing is not a true space frame. The
Italian term
*<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superleggera" id="mw5w"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Superleggera">Superleggera</a>* (meaning
'super-light') was trademarked for lightweight sports-car body
construction that still requires its own chassis, and thus only
resembles a space-frame chassis in general look and construction method.
It utilizes a
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_structures" id="mw6A"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Geodesic structures">geodesic</a>-like network of narrow tubes
running under the body, up the fenders and over the radiator, cowl, and
roof, and under the rear window, to provide form and attachment points
for a sheetmetal skin, typically aluminum for weight-savings, as
rigidity is not a consideration.

The terms "unibody" and "unit-body" are short for "unitized body",
"unitary construction", or alternatively (fully) integrated body and
frame/chassis. It is defined
as:<sup><a href="#cite_note-9" id="mw7Q"><span id="mw7g"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mw7w"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>9<span id="mw8A"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1244412712" typeof="mw:Extension/templatestyles mw:Transclusion" about="#mwt46" id="mw8Q" data-mw="{&quot;name&quot;:&quot;templatestyles&quot;,&quot;attrs&quot;:{&quot;src&quot;:&quot;Template:Blockquote/styles.css&quot;},&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Quote&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Quote&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;text&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;A type of body/frame construction in which the body of the vehicle, its floor pan and chassis form a single structure. Such a design is generally lighter and more rigid than a vehicle having a separate body and frame.&quot;},&quot;author&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;&quot;},&quot;title&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;&quot;},&quot;source&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 32px}.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;margin-top:0}@media(min-width:500px){.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{padding-left:1.6em}}</style>

> A type of body/frame construction in which the body of the vehicle,
> its floor pan and chassis form a single structure. Such a design is
> generally lighter and more rigid than a vehicle having a separate body
> and frame.

Vehicle structure has shifted from the traditional
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-on-frame" id="mw9A"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Body-on-frame">body-on-frame</a> architecture
to the lighter unitized/integrated body structure that is now used for
most cars.<sup><a href="#cite_note-10" id="mw9Q"><span id="mw9g"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mw9w"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>10<span id="mw-A"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

Integral frame and body construction requires more than simply welding
an unstressed body to a conventional frame. In a fully integrated body
structure, the entire car is a load-carrying unit that handles all the
loads experienced by the vehicle – forces from driving and cargo loads.
Integral-type bodies for wheeled vehicles are typically manufactured by
welding preformed metal panels and other components together, by forming
or casting whole sections as one piece, or by combining these
techniques. Although this is sometimes also referred to as a
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocoque" id="mw-g"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Monocoque">monocoque</a> structure, because the
car's outer skin and panels are made load-bearing, there are still ribs,
bulkheads, and box sections to reinforce the body, making the
description
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-monocoque" id="mw-w"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Semi-monocoque">semi-monocoque</a> more
appropriate.

The first attempt to develop such a design technique was on the 1922
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancia_Lambda" id="mw_Q"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Lancia Lambda">Lancia Lambda</a> to provide
structural stiffness and a lower body height for its
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_(car)" id="mw_g"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Torpedo (car)">torpedo car</a>
body.<sup><a href="#cite_note-Genta-11" id="mw_w"><span id="mwAQA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAQE"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>11<span id="mwAQI"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The Lambda had an open
layout with unstressed roof, which made it less of a *monocoque* shell
and more like a bowl. One thousand were
produced.<sup><a href="#cite_note-R&amp;T-12" id="mwAQQ"><span id="mwAQU"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAQY"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwAQc"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

A key role in developing the unitary body was played by the American
firm the Budd Company, now
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budd_Company#Automobiles"
id="mwAQk" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Budd Company">ThyssenKrupp Budd</a>.<sup><a href="#cite_note-R&amp;T-12" id="mwAQo"><span id="mwAQs"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAQw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwAQ0"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> Budd supplied
pressed-steel bodywork, fitted to separate frames, to automakers
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodge" id="mwAQ4"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Dodge">Dodge</a>,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Motor_Company" id="mwAQ8"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Ford Motor Company">Ford</a>,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buick" id="mwARA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Buick">Buick</a>, and the French company,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citroën" id="mwARE"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Citroën">Citroën</a>.

In 1930,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Ledwinka" id="mwARM"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Joseph Ledwinka">Joseph Ledwinka</a>, an
engineer with Budd, designed an automobile prototype with a full unitary
construction.<sup><a href="#cite_note-13" id="mwARQ"><span id="mwARU"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwARY"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>13<span id="mwARc"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citroën" id="mwARk"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Citroën">Citroën</a> purchased this fully
unitary body design for the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citroën_Traction_Avant"
id="mwARo" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Citroën Traction Avant">Citroën
Traction Avant</a>. This high-volume, mass-production car was introduced
in 1934 and sold 760,000 units over the next 23 years of
production.<sup><a href="#cite_note-R&amp;T-12" id="mwARs"><span id="mwARw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAR0"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwAR4"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> This application was the
first iteration of the modern structural integration of body and
chassis, using spot welded deeply stamped steel sheets into a structural
cage, including sills, pillars, and roof
beams.<sup><a href="#cite_note-Genta-11" id="mwAR8"><span id="mwASA"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwASE"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>11<span id="mwASI"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> In addition to a unitary
body with no separate frame, the Traction Avant also featured other
innovations such as
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front-wheel_drive" id="mwASM"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Front-wheel drive">front-wheel drive</a>. The
result was a low-slung vehicle with an open, flat-floored
interior.<sup><a href="#cite_note-14" id="mwASQ"><span id="mwASU"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwASY"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>14<span id="mwASc"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

For the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_Airflow" id="mwASk"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chrysler Airflow">Chrysler Airflow</a>
(1934–1937), Budd supplied a variation – three main sections from the
Airflow's body were welded into what Chrysler called a bridge-truss
construction. Unfortunately, this method was not ideal because the panel
fits were
poor.<sup><a href="#cite_note-R&amp;T-12" id="mwASo"><span id="mwASs"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwASw"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwAS0"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> To convince a skeptical
public of the strength of unibody, both Citroën and Chrysler created
advertising films showing cars surviving after being pushed off a
cliff.<sup><a href="#cite_note-R&amp;T-12" id="mwAS4"><span id="mwAS8"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwATA"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span id="mwATE"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opel" id="mwATM"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Opel">Opel</a> was the second European and the
first German car manufacturer to produce a car with a unibody structure
– production of the compact
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opel_Olympia" id="mwATQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Opel Olympia">Olympia</a> started in 1935. A
larger <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opel_Kapitän" id="mwATU"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Opel Kapitän">Kapitän</a> went into production
in 1938, although its front longitudinal beams were stamped separately
and then attached to the main body. It was so successful that the Soviet
post-war mass produced
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAZ-M20_Pobeda" id="mwATY"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="GAZ-M20 Pobeda">GAZ-M20 Pobeda</a> of 1946
copied unibody structure from the Opel
Kapitän.<sup><a href="#cite_note-15" id="mwATc"><span id="mwATg"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwATk"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>15<span id="mwATo"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> Later Soviet limousine
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAZ-12_ZIM" id="mwATs"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="GAZ-12 ZIM">GAZ-12 ZIM</a> of 1950 introduced
unibody design to automobiles with a wheelbase as long as 3.2 m (126
in).<sup><a href="#cite_note-16" id="mwATw"><span id="mwAT0"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAT4"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>16<span id="mwAT8"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

The streamlined 1936
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln-Zephyr" id="mwAUE"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Lincoln-Zephyr">Lincoln-Zephyr</a> with
conventional <a
href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front-engine,_rear-wheel-drive_layout"
id="mwAUI" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Front-engine, rear-wheel-drive layout">front-engine,
rear-wheel-drive layout</a> utilized a unibody
structure.<sup><a href="#cite_note-17" id="mwAUM"><span id="mwAUQ"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAUU"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>17<span id="mwAUY"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> By 1941, unit
construction was no longer a new idea for cars, "but it was unheard of
in the \[American\] low-price field \[and\]
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_Motors" id="mwAUc"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Nash Motors">Nash</a> wanted a bigger share of
that market."<sup><a href="#cite_note-18" id="mwAUg"><span id="mwAUk"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAUo"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>18<span id="mwAUs"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-19" id="mwAUw"><span id="mwAU0"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAU4"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>19<span id="mwAU8"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> The single unit-body
construction of the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_600" id="mwAVA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Nash 600">Nash 600</a> provided weight savings
and Nash's Chairman and CEO,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Mason" id="mwAVE"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="George W. Mason">George W. Mason</a> was
convinced "that unibody was the wave of the
future."<sup><a href="#cite_note-20" id="mwAVI"><span id="mwAVM"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAVQ"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>20<span id="mwAVU"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a><a href="#cite_note-Narus-21" id="mwAVY"><span id="mwAVc"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAVg"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>21<span id="mwAVk"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

Since then, more cars were redesigned to the unibody structure, which is
now "considered standard in the
industry".<sup><a href="#cite_note-Narus-21" id="mwAVs"><span id="mwAVw"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAV0"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>21<span id="mwAV4"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup> By 1960, the unitized
body design was used by Detroit's Big Three on their compact cars
(<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Falcon_(North_America)"
id="mwAV8" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Ford Falcon (North America)">Ford
Falcon</a>,
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plymouth_Valiant" id="mwAWA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Plymouth Valiant">Plymouth Valiant</a>, and
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Corvair" id="mwAWE"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chevrolet Corvair">Chevrolet Corvair</a>).
After Nash merged with Hudson Motors to form
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Motors_Corporation"
id="mwAWI" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="American Motors Corporation">American Motors Corporation</a>, its
Rambler-badged automobiles continued exclusively building variations of
the unibody.

Although the 1934
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_Airflow" id="mwAWQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chrysler Airflow">Chrysler Airflow</a> had a
weaker-than-usual frame and body framework welded to the chassis to
provide stiffness, in 1960, Chrysler moved from body-on-frame
construction to a unit-body design for most of its
cars.<sup><a href="#cite_note-22" id="mwAWU"><span id="mwAWY"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAWc"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>22<span id="mwAWg"
class="cite-bracket">]</span></span></a></sup>

Most of the American-manufactured unibody automobiles used
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque_boxes" id="mwAWo"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Torque boxes">torque
boxes</a> in their vehicle design to reduce vibrations and chassis flex,
except for the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevy_II" id="mwAWs"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chevy II">Chevy II</a>,
which had a bolt-on front apron (erroneously referred to as a
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subframe" id="mwAWw"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Subframe">subframe</a>).

The unibody is now the preferred construction for mass-market
automobiles. This design provides weight savings, improved space
utilization, and ease of manufacture. The rapid development of
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_method" id="mwAW4"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Finite element method">finite element
analysis</a> (FEA) methods in the 1970s has allowed engineers to easily
optimise unibody structures for strength, crash protection and minimal
material use. Acceptance grew dramatically in the wake of the two
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_crises" id="mwAW8"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Energy crises">energy
crises</a> of the 1970s and that of the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000s_energy_crisis" id="mwAXA"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="2000s energy crisis">2000s</a> in which compact
SUVs using a truck platform (primarily the USA market) were subjected to
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_average_fuel_economy"
id="mwAXE" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Corporate average fuel economy">CAFE
standards</a> after 2005 (by the late 2000s truck-based compact SUVs
were phased out and replaced with crossovers). An additional advantage
of a strong-bodied car lies in the improved crash protection for its
passengers.

#### Uniframe

<span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\[</span>[edit](/w/index.php?title=Vehicle_frame&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Uniframe")<span class="mw-editsection-bracket">\]</span></span>

American Motors (with its partner
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renault" id="mwAXQ"

incorporated unibody construction when designing the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeep_Cherokee_(XJ)" id="mwAXU"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Jeep Cherokee (XJ)">Jeep Cherokee (XJ)</a>
platform using the manufacturing principles (unisides, floorplan with
integrated frame rails and crumple zones, and roof panel) used in its
passenger cars, such as the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_Hornet" id="mwAXY"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="AMC Hornet">Hornets</a> and all-wheel-drive
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_Eagle" id="mwAXc"

with the weight advantages of Unibody

A subframe is a distinct structural frame component, to reinforce or
complement a particular section of a vehicle's structure. Typically
attached to a unibody or a monocoque, the rigid subframe can handle
great forces from the engine and drive train. It can transfer them
evenly to a wide area of relatively thin sheet metal of a unitized body
shell. Subframes are often found at the front or rear end of cars and
are used to attach the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_(vehicle)" id="mwAY0"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Suspension (vehicle)">suspension</a> to the vehicle. A subframe
may also contain the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine#Modern" id="mwAY4"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Engine">engine</a> and
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_(mechanics)"
id="mwAY8" class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink"
title="Transmission (mechanics)">transmission</a>. It normally has
pressed or box steel construction but may be tubular and/or other
material.

Examples of passenger car use include the 1967–1981
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GM_F_platform" id="mwAZE"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="GM F platform">GM F
platform</a>, the numerous years and models built on the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GM_X_platform_(1962)" id="mwAZI"
class="mw-redirect" rel="mw:WikiLink" title="GM X platform (1962)">GM X
platform (1962)</a>, GM's M/L platform vans
(<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Astro" id="mwAZM"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="Chevrolet Astro">Chevrolet Astro</a>/GMC
Safari, which included an all-wheel drive variant), and the unibody
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_Pacer" id="mwAZQ"
rel="mw:WikiLink" title="AMC Pacer">AMC Pacer</a> that incorporated a
front subframe to isolate the passenger compartment from the engine,
suspension, and steering
loads.<sup><a href="#cite_note-24" id="mwAZU"><span id="mwAZY"
class="mw-reflink-text"><span id="mwAZc"
class="cite-bracket">[</span>24<span id="mwAZg"
